What a file is, for any app and any company: its type, its properties, its history

A brief for Miky · revised 26 Oct 2026 after your four notes, your message on interoperability, and your notes on spam and the App Store · replaces the version sent earlier today · it would replace the two Proposed records in the draft estiva-docs#330 · one architecture call, then three calls it brings with it

The question

What should a file be on the wire, so that any app opens it fast, an HR expert can build an HR app without learning Nostr, two companies can share a project and still understand each other's files, people can change properties as easily as in Notion, and every file keeps its history?

Your goals: 1 fast for any app, on first load and in sync; 2 a simple Notion: files with properties, and simple databases; 3 a new app needs no new kinds; 4 easy migration when properties change; 5 people can see a file's history; and, new today, 6 two companies connect and share a Folder, as in Slack Connect.

What changed since the first version

  • "Nostr is for interoperability, less for decentralization" (your message today). This sorts the Nostr community's reasons in two. Some are about many relays and unknown clients, and they weigh less for us. Others are about apps understanding each other, and they weigh more for us. The second kind is the one that decides this brief.
  • "They probably had good reason." They did, and it is an interoperability reason: a type is shared only if it is named for a meaning, not for an app. A new section below sets out how they think about kinds at scale.
  • Slack Connect is now goal 6. Each option is re-weighed for a Folder that two companies' relays both serve. There is a new picture of the two ways that can work.
  • Builders are domain experts. There is a new "Builder" column: what an HR expert must learn under each option.
  • The recommendation changed, from C to a new option E: shared types. Common meanings, such as an issue or a candidate, get one number each, as Nostr asks, and any app extends them with its own properties. Everything not shared yet is a record. The relay keeps each file's current state, as in C. C's types were named for apps, and that is the one pattern this research shows the community turning down for interoperability's sake.

Today

A file is one of four types: a topic, a document, a project, an issue. Each has its own number on the wire, and each is named for what it is, not for an app. A new type needs Estiva to change three things: the list of what each app may sign in Estiva ID, the relay's code, and a relay deploy. An outside company cannot add a type.

Everything about a file that changes (its title, status, assignee, and its whole text) is written as a small change: "set this field of this file to this value". The newest change per field wins, and every app works out a file's current state by reading all of its changes, ever. Fields are named by their word, with no separate id. The text is one of those fields, so each save is a full copy in the change log. Measured today: text saves weigh 33.2 MB, and all other changes 0.95 MB. That is why Weekly Plans took 30–35 s to open on 25 Oct. Every change is kept, but no app shows a history.

A Folder lives on one company's relay and cannot move. The relay already serves several companies, one per web address, each with its own Folders.

Decided before. A document is its own type of file, with its own number (you, 14 Oct). Each text save is a full readable copy, and live co-editing comes later, on top (13 Oct). On 25 Oct you said an app may add types (for now only an app, not a person), and old shapes are translated when read. That became the two Proposed records in the draft #330, now on hold.

Why it reaches beyond this ticket

  • It sets what a file is for every app and every company, and what an outside builder must learn to join: numbers, or a listing.
  • It sets whether two companies' apps understand each other's files when they share a Folder. That is the reason you chose Nostr.
  • One-way: how a type is named (a number per meaning, a name each app picks, or a number each app picks), and how a property is named (its word, or a fixed id). Once another company's files sit on the relay under one scheme, changing it means moving files signed by people we cannot sign for.
  • Two-way: who works out a file's current state. While the change log stays the truth, a summary kept by the relay can be added or dropped later. Without it, apps fold the log themselves: slower, but still correct.

How the Nostr community thinks about kinds, and what applies to us

At scale, they do not expect every app to have its own number. They expect one number per meaning, shared by every app that handles it. Data no other app needs to read goes in a generic app-data container that is honest about not being shared. Nobody hands out numbers: 281 are registered, by pull request, and collisions happen (we found four, one of them with our Folder listing's number). Proposals to use names or namespaces instead of numbers were turned down twice. Whether apps really reuse numbers by meaning in practice is not confirmed: there are anecdotes both ways.

So the line that matters is not "numbers or names". It is "named for a meaning and shared" against "named for an app". Options B and C as first drafted sit on the wrong side of it. That is a protocol-shape problem, not a matter of optics, once a partner company's apps must read our files.

Options

Three things vary: what names a type (a number each app picks; a name each app picks; or a number per shared meaning), how much the relay does (stores events; keeps each file's current state; knows every schema), and, new, what a partner company's apps understand. In every option, the text moves out of the change log into an event of its own.

A. Do less: a number per type, each app picks its own, made cheap to add

What changes. Text saves become their own events. Apps keep a copy on the device and ask only for what is new. An app the workspace has added may sign the numbers its listing declares, with no Estiva step. Properties stay named by their word.

What you and Katerina see. Fast again today, since the change log without text is under 1 MB. A second open is instant in any app that keeps a copy.

Builder. The HR expert picks numbers for Job position and Candidate in the right range (the range decides how the event is stored), picks which one-letter tags to use so fields can be filtered, avoids numbers others took, and builds a device copy and a fold. No other app understands those types unless the builder writes a standard and other apps adopt it.

Two companies. The partner's relay must admit every number of every app used in the shared Folder. Files are understood only where both sides run the same app.

What could go wrong. Collisions (one already, with our Folder listing). First load on a new phone, for a new colleague or for Claude grows with every edit ever made. A Notion-style database still needs a typed row.

B. One kind of file whose types each app names; every app folds the history itself (the draft #330)

What changes. One kind of file. Its root names its type under the app that declares it ("Ship's issue", "the HR app's candidate"). The listing declares each type's properties with a fixed id, a label and a kind of value. Apps fold the change log, with a device copy.

What you and Katerina see. The same speed as A. Renaming a property or a stage is instant and touches no file.

Builder. Writes a listing, with no numbers. Still builds a device copy and a fold to be fast.

Two companies. The partner's relay admits one kind. The partner's own tracker sees "Ship's issue" as a stranger, not as an issue.

What could go wrong. First load grows with every edit, so history must be cut to stay fast. Each app's fold disagrees on odd cases. Today's 937 files move to the one kind, re-signed by their authors.

C. As B, and the relay keeps each file's current state (the first version's pick)

What changes. Everything in B, plus this: when a change arrives, the relay applies the rule every app uses today, "the newest change per field wins". It keeps one small signed summary per file: type, title, Folder, each property's newest value with who set it and when, and the newest text save. Lists read one summary per file. The change log becomes the history. The relay does not know what fields mean.

What you and Katerina see. Any list opens with one small read per file, however long its history. History shows every change, because nothing has to be deleted to keep lists fast. A row can say "Katerina set Stage to Offer, 2 h ago".

Builder. Writes a listing and reads summaries. Has no fold or sync engine to build.

Two companies. If both relays serve the Folder, each works out its own summaries from the same changes. That works only because the rule is generic. The types are still named for apps, so a partner's tracker still sees strangers.

What could go wrong. The relay's rule becomes part of the protocol and must stay generic for good. A change claiming a far-future time would win for ever unless the relay clamps it. Our relay drifts further from upstream Buzz ("the pipe, not the brain"). And the community turns down this pattern by name: "No. Use a new kind number for the thing you want to do" (fiatjaf, July 2026).

A variant that keeps the relay simple: each field's newest value becomes its own replaceable event, as the Kanbanstr and Formstr apps do. It works on any relay, but costs two writes per edit and many more events per file.

D. Contrarian: the relay is the workspace's database

What changes. The relay reads every type's schema, refuses a write that does not fit, answers queries ("candidates in 1st interview, assigned to Katerina") and serves each file converted to today's schema. Apps are thin views, as with Notion's and Linear's servers.

What you and Katerina see. The fastest database views at any size, and a wrong value refused the moment it is typed.

Builder. Writes a listing and nothing else. But a new kind of property, such as a currency or a formula, waits for our relay release.

Two companies. The worst of the five. Both relays must know both companies' schemas, at the same version, or a shared Folder accepts a write on one side and refuses it on the other.

What could go wrong. A schema bug in the relay breaks every app at once. It overturns the heart of an Accepted record ("share the wire format, never the interpretation"). It is the most to build and to run.

E. Shared types: one number per shared meaning, extended by any app; the relay keeps each file's current state (recommended)

What changes. Three layers, and C's relay summary under all of them:

  • Shared types. A meaning many apps use: an issue, a project, a document, a topic today; later perhaps a task, a contact, a job position, a candidate. Each has its own number, as Nostr asks, and a short public definition of its core properties, each with a fixed id and a label (an issue's status, and what each status means). Who curates them is call 2.
  • Extensions. Any app adds its own properties to a shared type in its listing: the HR app adds "Salary band" to Job position. Other apps show them as plain values under the HR app's label, or hide them. No new number.
  • Records for everything not shared yet. One kind with one meaning: "an item whose type is defined by the schema it names". That schema is a row's database, which a person made, or an app's own type, such as the HR app's "Interview scorecard". Nostr's comment kind works the same way: one meaning that names what it hangs on. Other apps show a record plainly, with its properties. When several apps want a record type, it becomes a shared type and its files move once.

Today's four types already are one number per meaning, named for the meaning. They stay where they are, their field words become their ids, and nobody re-signs anything. The relay admits a whole block of numbers set aside for shared types, so a new one needs no relay release. An added app signs the types its listing declares.

What you and Katerina see. Everything C gives: one read per file, full history, "who set this" on every row. And when a partner's project manager opens the shared project in their own tracker, Ship's issues are issues there: "done" means done.

Builder. The HR expert writes a listing: "Job position: the shared type, plus Salary band and Hiring manager. Interview scorecard: our own record type." No numbers, no tag letters, no Estiva step. Their job positions show in any app that knows job positions. To make "candidate" shared, they propose it.

Two companies. The partner's relay admits the shared numbers and the record kind, the same set on every relay. Shared meanings are understood across both companies' apps, which is the point of interoperability. Each relay can work out its own summaries, as in C.

A Nostr developer reads the community's own rule: one number per meaning, shared across apps. The record kind is the one place where a type sits inside a kind. We say plainly that records are not for sharing, as the community asks of app data.

What could go wrong.

  • Curation is a job that never ends. Someone must say no to a second "task" and yes to "candidate", and decide quickly. If that is slow, everything stays a record, and we are back to app-named types. The optional App Store's review helps people agree, but it forces no one.
  • A breaking change to a shared type is a new number. Old and new live side by side until files move. That is Nostr's rule and AT Protocol's.
  • Promoting a record type to a shared type re-signs its files under their authors. Files of an author who has left stay records.
  • A shared type has no owning app, so "the app that owns a kind makes the file" becomes "any added app whose listing declares the type".
  • An existing Nostr kind may cover a meaning with another shape. Nostr has a git-issue kind, and a listing kind that covers job posts. We reuse one where its shape fits, or say why we don't.

A variant: the same layers as names on one kind of file ("issue", "candidate"). Builders would see no difference. Not recommended: it is the pattern the community turns down by name, the type leaves the file's address (a turned-down option in an Accepted record), and names invite the first-come land grab Vitor warns about.

Two companies, one Folder

There are two ways a Folder can live on two companies' relays. Which one is its own decision, with its own brief, but the file shape has to work under both.

  • What every option needs under both: the partner's relay admits the kinds used in the Folder. Under A, that is every app's numbers. Under B and C, one kind. Under E, the shared block and the record kind, the same set everywhere.
  • Under "on both", each relay works out its own summaries from the same changes. That works only if the rule stays generic and gives the same answer from the same changes. This is one more reason to lock in "generic, for good" (C and E), and the reason D breaks: both relays would need both companies' schemas.
  • What only E gives: the partner's own apps understand the files. Under A, B and C, sharing works only where both companies run the same app.
  • Left for the Slack Connect brief: hosted or on both; whose admins govern; how an Acme member is let in on our relay; whose retention applies (in Slack, each company's retention applies to its own members' messages, and only the sender's company can edit or delete them). And the Accepted "Estiva hosts the only deployment": today two companies are two communities on our one relay deployment, which is the easier case. A company running its own relay would be a new decision.

How others do it

  • Nostr: one number per meaning, shared across apps; a generic container for app-only data; one comment kind that names what it comments on. Group relays sign group state (NIP-29), as we do for Folder listings. A group is "this relay, this id": it can be moved or forked, but no Nostr proposal has one group governed jointly on two relays.
  • AT Protocol (Bluesky): each app names its record types under its own domain, with no approval. A breaking change needs a new name. To extend someone else's type, an app writes a sidecar record next to it. Shared meanings are curated by a separate volunteer group with a steering committee (the Lexicon Community, e.g. calendar events). For us: closest to E. Their app-named types are the default and shared ones came later; E starts from shared ones.
  • schema.org: one public vocabulary (JobPosting, Person, Event) curated by a steering group, which sites extend. For us: the model for curating shared types in public.
  • Notion and Airtable: properties have fixed ids, so renames are free. The server serves current state and converts values on a type change. History is kept by plan. For us: one company owns every type and may rewrite anything; we cannot rewrite changes other people signed.
  • Linear: the server serves state with a sync cursor and writes history rows as changes land. For us: the model for C's and E's summary.
  • Anytype: types and properties are objects people create in their space. For us: the model for a person's database as a file.
  • Slack Connect and Teams shared channels: one channel, one copy. In Teams the host company owns the data; in Slack each company sets retention for its own members' messages. Matrix: every server in a room keeps a full copy, and all of them run the same rule to agree on members and admins. That rule has been Matrix's hardest problem. For us: the two Slack Connect models above.

Recommendation

Pick E: one number for each shared meaning, which any app extends with its own properties; one record kind for everything not shared yet; and a relay that keeps each file's current state while the change log becomes its history.

  • The protocol shape. You use Nostr for interoperability. Interoperability needs types named for meanings and shared across apps, never named for one app. That is the community's rule, and it is about interoperability, not social media. E follows it in full. The parts of their reasoning about many relays and unknown clients (indexing, per-relay rules) weigh less on one company relay, spam does not apply at all, and E does not lean on any of them.
  • The person. Katerina, and a partner's project manager in another tool, see the same issue with the same meaning. An HR expert writes a listing and never meets a number. A new colleague, a new phone and Claude read one summary per file, however long its history. History is complete, because nothing is deleted to keep lists fast.
  • Not C: its types are named by each app, so two companies' different apps never agree what an issue is. It is also the one pattern the community turns down by name. C's relay summary carries over into E unchanged.
  • Not A: every builder picks numbers, ranges and tag letters (your note 4), and the numbers still name apps, not meanings.
  • Not B: C without the summary, so it must cut history to stay fast.
  • Not D: every relay in a shared Folder would need every company's schemas.
  • Cost, said plainly: E is less to build than C now. Today's four types already are shared types, so no file moves. It adds a lasting job, curating shared types (call 2). Staging, without changing the shape: text out first (every option needs it); ids and labels for today's four types, and app gates driven by listings; the relay summary; records and people's databases with the Notion-like app; Slack Connect after its own brief.

Not asked now

  • How a person reshapes a database (a property changed from text to choice; options merged). It is shaped with the Notion-like app. The rule that holds from now on is fixed ids with labels, so a rename touches no file.
  • Slack Connect's own shape (hosted, or on both relays). It is the next brief. E works under both.
  • A file's text. It follows your earlier calls. Each save is its own event; the summary names the newest. Until someone saves again, each file's newest old save is read as its text, so nobody re-signs (your 26 Oct lean). Sections and live co-editing come later; a CRDT as the stored text stays turned down (13 Oct).
